Greenhouse gas forcing, aerosol forcing, and cloud feedback all contribute to this increase. However, the role of aerosol forcing, particularly effective radiativeforcing through aerosol-cloud inter- actions (ERFACI), remains highly uncertain and closely intertwined with cloud feedback. Here, we estimate shortwave ERFACI using satellite observations and show its importance as a contributor to the EEI increase between 2003 and 2023. ERFACI exhibits a signiﬁcant increasing trend of 0.33 Wm−2/decade averaged over oceans between 60∘S and 60∘N. The increasing trend of ERFACI stems from a global decline in cloud droplet number concentration driven by decreasing anthropogenic aerosol emissions. It is of similar magnitude as the combined instantaneous forcing trend from greenhouse gases and aerosol- radiation interactions. Our results mayreduce the gap between the simulated and observed shortwave contribution to EEI trends while implying a weak shortwave cloud feedback. They also suggest a stronger aerosol forcing than the multi-model mean. The Earth’s energy imbalance (EEI) is the difference between absorbed solar radiation and emitted outgoing longwave radiation1,2. A positive EEI indicates an Earth that is gaining heat and leads to higher global mean temperature, sea level rise, and melting of ice and snow. EEI is the net effect of all processes that affect the Earth’s energy budget. Reduced outgoing longwave radiation by greenhouse gases increases EEI and heats up the atmosphere and ocean, which triggers changes in variables such as global mean surface temperature, cloud radiative effect, water vapor, and sur- face albedo which in turn affect EEI (see“Methods”)3,4. Anthro- pogenic aerosols can affect EEI through direct changes to the absorbed solar radiation, i.e., the net solarﬂux into the climate system, and indirectly modifying cloud properties, a combined effect known as total effective radiative forcing (ERF) from aerosols. The magnitude of total aerosol forcing is potentially large, but remains highly uncertain5,6. Internal climate variations, and changes in natural aerosol and solar irradiance can also affect EEI, but their long-term contribution is much smaller1– 3,7. An estimated 90% of EEI goes toward increased ocean heat con- tent, while the remainder melts snow and ice and increases land and atmosphere temperature2,3,8. Measurements of ocean heat content show a consistent positive trend since the 1960s, and an accelerated trend during the last two decades9. Satellite observations from NASA’s Clouds and the Earth’s Radiant Energy System (CERES) instrument10 conﬁrm this positive trend of 0.63 Wm−2/decade for the period 2003– 2023 over global oceans between 60oSa n d6 0oN( 0 . 5 1 W m−2/ decade for the whole globe)3,11. Together, these independent records provide a top-down constraint on the combined effect of forcing, feedback, and internal variability in shaping Earth’s evolving energy budget. For example, partial radiative perturbation analysis demonstrates that clouds, water vapor, trace gases, surface albedo changes, and global mean temperature all affect EEI trends11,12. The radiative impact of clouds plays a major role in increasing EEI3,11. However, separating the effects of cloud feedback— tempera- ture-mediated radiative response -6,7,14 from ERFACI remains difﬁcult because both are uncertain and intertwined. Cloud feedback remains a key uncertainty in climate feedback studies, while ERFACI is the leading source of uncertainty in radiative forcing estimates5,6. This is now possible through well-calibrated, long-term satellite observations of clouds and aero- sols and reanalysis data, along with recent methodological advances in obtaining observed ERFACI 16– 20.N A S A’s Moderate Resolution Ima- ging Spectroradiometer (MODIS) instrument provides global retrie- vals of cloud variables such as cloud droplet effective radius, cloud optical depth, and cloud fraction21. At the same time, opportunistic experiments of aerosol-cloud interactions offer quantitative char- acterization of cloud adjustments to aerosols as a function of back- ground conditions22,23. Such cloud adjustment functions can be used to build bottom-up estimates of ERFACI that are calibrated against independent estimates from top-down approaches and modeling studies (see Methods)19,24,25. Because the calibration of Aqua MODIS is remarkably stable26, trends derived from it capture actual changes rather than instrumental drifts, before the satellite orbit itself started drifting. Moreover, anthropogenic aerosol emissions and aerosol loading have undergone substantial regional and global changes during the same period (see Figs. S1 and S2)27. Changes in aerosol emissions affect cloud formation and leave detectable trends in cloud droplet number concentration (Nd) at both regional and global scales (Figs.1 and S2)28, providing an opportunity to study the resulting ERFACI trend. This study quantiﬁes the ERFACI trend between 2003 and 2023 using observations, assesses its importance relative to other pro- cesses in the context of EEI increase, and discusses its implications. Most results reported here pertain to global oceans between 60oS and 60oN because our cloud adjustment functions have been derived from samples within this domain (see Methods for more details)23. Discussion and conclusions The fact that ERFACI can rival instantaneous greenhouse gas forcing over decadal timescales according to our analysis has important implications for understanding the past and predicting future decadal climate changes. The decline in global mean temperature from the 1940s to the 1970s coincided with a steep rise in anthropogenic sulfur emissions (Fig. S2), whereas the accelerated warming since the 1980s aligns with a reversal of this trend (Figs. S1 and S6 and refs.43,44). Given the accelerated increase in emissions between the 1940s and 1970s, the resulting ERFACI change was likely stronger than in the past two decades— potentially enough to temporarily overpower green- house gas forcing and cause net cooling. The warming effect of ERFACI (Fig. 2) during the last decade contributes to the EEI increase, which conspires with other factors in driving the rapid warming rate in recent years (Fig.2,19,25,33,36). Looking ahead, the ongoing and projected future decline in aerosol emissions, coupled with rising greenhouse gas for- cing, is expected to amplify near-future warming. Moreover, the‘pat- tern effect’of cloud feedback may have dampened warming since the 1980s; if this effect weakens or reverses in the future, it could further increase the future warming rate45– 47 The reduction in anthropogenic aerosol emissions in the past two decades is effectively an inadvertent global scale reverse geoengi- neering event19, which has contributed substantial warming effect based on our estimates and whose cost must be considered. While the shift to cleaner energy sources and strict air quality measures beneﬁt human health, it also reduces aerosol loading which exacerbates the rate of warming. For example, the shipping fuel regulations of 2020 have likely introduced additional warming19,25 with a range of possible adverse impacts. Should the costs of rapid warming prove substantial, research and evaluation of short-term solar radiation modiﬁcation strategies such as marine cloud brightening need to be better understood48 as a companion option to the long-term goal of reduced greenhouse gas emissions. The strong ERFACI trend in this study indi- cates that aerosol forcing is close to the high end of model ensemble estimates and solar radiation modiﬁcation strategies may be effective at temporarily reducing warming rate even though they have other consequences. It is important to note that estimating ERFACI remains challenging due to various sources of uncertainty5. For example, MODIS estimates of Nd can be biased under certain observational conditions. When exploring the sensitivity of ourﬁndings to the details ofﬁltering MODIS data at the pixel level49 we found that application ofﬁlters tends to produce stronger negative trends inNd and therefore higher ERFACI estimates (Figs. S3 and S4). Different approaches of estimating ERFACI can also produce different results. For example, multi-model mean of ERFACI suggests a weaker trend at around 0.06 ± 0.06 Wm−2/ decade between 2001 and 202015 while other observation-based esti- mates suggest even stronger ERFACI than ours17,23. Recent studies also show diverging results regarding the magnitude of ERFACI’sc o n t r i b u - tion to the recent EEI increase, from near zero31 to substantial (like our estimate)50, suggesting that ERFACI is still uncertain depending on the methods and models (see Table1). The small uncertainty of the trend reported here thus should not be interpreted as the uncertainty for ERFACI is reduced. Instead, this only reﬂects the uncertainty in our own method that has several assumptions that may reduce the uncertainty range (see“Method”). More work is needed to take advantage of the observational constraints in the last two decades and further improve ERFACI characterization. There is a need for reliable ERFACI estimates over land trends to better calculate their true global impact. The strong ERFACI and its hemispheric asymmetry also have implications for understanding recent shifts in warming pattern in the Paciﬁc. Assumptions. We note a few assumptions and simpliﬁcations made in our method. The LW effect of aerosol-cloud interactions in low clouds is not considered. This effect is of secondary importance due to the small temperature contrast between low clouds and surface, with a previous study suggesting that it accounts for roughly 10% of the SW ERFACI 6,17. The cloud adjustments used here apply best to the strato- cumulus regime because most ship-tracks form in this cloud regime, but we assume that they also apply to trade cumulus clouds as well. Comparing results from our method with previous studies suggests that our method estimates smaller cloud fraction changes in response toNd than other published studies for the trade cumulus clouds65,66, which would make our estimated ERFACI for these clouds a lower bound. Cloud adjustments to aerosols can evolve signiﬁcantly with time. The adjustments derived from ship-tracks represent a time- average behavior which may not be strictly representative because ship-track clouds have different ages67. It is also important to note that unlike modeling estimates ofERFACI that include effects of atmo- spheric responses to aerosol changes our method only considers cloud responses to microphysical changes. Other assumptions and uncertainties are discussed in ref.23. Radiative kernel calculations. Widely used in the decomposition of radiative changes, radiative kernels are a set of pre-computed partial derivatives representing the sensitivity of radiation to a perturbation in as p e c iﬁc state variable. As referred to in the section above, radiative kernels are used in this work to diagnose the non-cloud and cloud radiative feedback and adjustment terms contributing to totalΔRλ and subsequently to diagnose the aerosol direct forcing as a residual cal- culation. The non-cloud terms (ΔRnon−clouds) include feedbacks and adjustments in temperature (ΔRT), water vapor (ΔRq) and surface albedo (ΔRa). Following68 and other work cited herein, each of these non-cloud terms can be diagnosed as the product of a radiative kernel (Kx)a n dt h ea c t u a lc h a n g ei nv a r i a b l ex : ΔRnon/C0 clouds = X ΔRx /C25 X ∂R ∂x × Δx /C18/C19 /C25 X ðKx × ΔxÞ ð12Þ where a clear-sky and all-sky Kx allows one to diagnose clear- and all- sky ΔRx, respectively. While theseΔRx terms are not directly a focus of this work, the difference betweenthe all-sky and clear-sky terms provide an estimate of the Cloud Masking (CM) effect needed to derive the cloud radiative feedback and adjustment term (ΔRc), as noted in the section above (Eqs. S8 and S9). The kernel-derived estimates ofΔRx are also used to diagnose the aerosol direct radiative forcing as a residual calculation. Namely, the clear-sky aerosol direct forcing is estimated as the difference between the clear-skyΔASR and the sum of the clear-sky, shortwaveΔRx terms. Then, the all-sky IRF presented in this work is derived by scaling the clear-sky IRF by a constant that accounts for cloud masking13,68. Each of these terms can be diagnosed from satellite observations over the CERES period. We extend previous estimates of these terms by ref.13, similarly applying CloudSat-based radiative kernels69 to satellite-observed anomalies of surface albedo from CERES-EBAF Ed 4.2, and water vapor and temperature anomalies from AIRS-CLIMCAPS V270 to diagnose the non-cloud radiative responses. CERES-EBAF Ed 4.2 is a more recent edition compared to what was used in ref.13 and CLIMCAPS serves an alternative to the since-discontinued AIRS V6 operational product. As in ref.13, the clear-sky IRF is diagnosed as the difference between clear-sky net radiative anomalies from CERES and the sum of clear-sky radiative responses. Then, the all-sky IRF pre- sented in this work is derived by scaling the clear-sky IRF by a constant that accounts for cloud masking13,68 The small, red blotch in Fig.3d along the Antarctic Coast directly South of South Africa is likely an artifact of using radiative kernels to diagnose the ARI. By deﬁnition, a radiative kernel assumes linearity in the sensitivity of radiation to the perturbation in a given state variable. This holds well for nearly all use cases, but not so well for the appli- cation of surface albedo kernels over sea-ice, which has a dependence on the base state. For instance, although the kernel assumes the sen- sitivity of SW radiation to an albedo change to be aﬁxed sensitivity, in reality shortwave radiation becomes less sensitive to a given albedo change as the underlying surface warms, ice melts, and local clouds thicken71. This can lead to biases in estimates of the surface-albedo radiative feedback when the constant kernel is being applied to an albedo perturbation that occurs under ice and atmospheric conditions different enough from those used to originally develop the kernel. Since the ARI forcing estimate is a residual calculation, any bias in the estimate of the surface-albedo radiative feedback will, in turn, lead to a bias in the residual calculation of the forcing. When applied to obser- vations, as in this work, the bias is localized over a small area and has negligible impact on the global-mean ARI forcing estimate13. Scaling results to global mean.T h eE R FACI results reported here are for oceans between 60oSa n d6 0oN because cloud adjustment func- tions are derived based on oceanic clouds. To assess their effective global mean impact, we scale them by the ratio of ocean area between 60oS and 60oNt oE a r t h’s surface area, which is 0.63. This effectively assumes ERFACI trends to be 0 everywhere else. Another option to calculate global mean is to include only the Twomey effect over land and assume cloud adjustments from LWP and CF to be zero since we do not have observations, and then scale the resulting forcing by the area ratio of 60oSa n d6 0oN to the globe. We call the means under this assumption“equivalent global means". For hemispheric contrast in a variable, we similarly scale a hemi- spheric value by the ratio between the ocean area between 0o and 60o in this hemisphere by half of Earth’s surface area. This captures the contribution from ocean areas to that hemisphere. The hemispheric contrast of such scaled values therefore represents the contribution of ocean areas to the full hemispheric contrast. We report these values as “effective" hemispheric contrasts.
