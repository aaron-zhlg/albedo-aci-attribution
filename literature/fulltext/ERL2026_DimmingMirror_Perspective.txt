E-mail: fredrik.c.l@historia.su.se Keywords:cloud–surface feedbacks, earth energy imbalance, grassland climate amplification Abstract Earth has reflected progressively less solar radiation since the start of the satellite era (1979), ampli- fying the planetary energy imbalance. Satellite analyses attribute the trend primarily to circulation- driven contraction of reflective cloud regimes, whereas process-based studies emphasize surface reflectivity changes. We argue that these perspectives are dynamically linked: as cloud cover con- tracts, diminishing atmospheric masking elevates the realized climatic leverage of surface albedo. This coupling makes surface reflectivity an increasingly important and manageable regulator of solar absorption under clearer-sky conditions. We propose an integrated governance framework that explicitly incorporates albedo management alongside carbon management. 1.Agrowingenergyimbalanceandthe needforacoherentsurface–albedo framework Satellite observations from the Clouds and the Earth’s Radiant Energy System (CERES) indicate that the Earth’s energy imbalance—the net differ- ence between absorbed solar energy and outgo- ing flux—has approximately doubled since the early 2000s, increasing from ∼0.5 to ∼1.0 W m−2 [1]. Independent ocean-heat content records confirm this acceleration, documenting an exceptional rise in planetary heat uptake since the onset of the satel- lite era in 1979 and the advent of high-precision CERES monitoring in 2000 [ 2, 3]. Early multi- decadal heat inventories established a baseline plan- etary imbalance [4] that has been decisively exceeded during the 1993–2023 period. Contemporary obser- vations suggest that this amplification is partly asso- ciated with the progressive unmasking of greenhouse forcing as anthropogenic aerosol cooling declines [5]. A central implication of this ‘clearing-sky’ phase (characterized by declining aerosol optical depth, reductions in low-cloud cover, and cryosphere reflectivity loss) is that a larger fraction of incoming shortwave radiation now reaches the surface. As a result, surface albedo exerts increasing climatic lever- age as reflective cloud cover contracts. Although the global imbalance remains the primary metric for tracking the climate trajectory, its spatial expres- sion is highly heterogeneous and can be amplified at the biome scale. In grassland systems, for exam- ple, the imbalance has intensified from∼0.8 to over 1.7 W m−2 (figure 1). T ogether, these observations document a transition from the near-equilibrium state of the 1970s toward a regime of sustained and amplified energy accumulation. These regional anomalies coincide with a pronounced ‘clearing- sky’ phase associated with declining aerosol optical depth, reductions in low-cloud cover, and shifts in land-surface properties that together enhance surface shortwave absorption [1, 5, 6]. Additional contribu- tions arise from cryosphere reflectivity with sea-ice and snow loss [7]. Despite broad agreement on the observed decline in planetary reflectivity, attribution remains frag- mented. Recent satellite observations from the Amazon further demonstrate that cloud- mediated increases in top-of-atmosphere (TOA) albedo associated with forest loss can approximately double the cooling effect relative to surface bright- ening alone, underscoring the importance of coupled cloud–surface feedbacks in Earth’s energy balance [8]. Here we argue that these perspectives are dynami- cally linked: as cloud cover contracts, a larger fraction of incoming shortwave radiation reaches the surface, increasing the climatic leverage of surface albedo and land-surface processes under clearer-sky conditions. 2.Cloud-regimecontraction:aleadingyet incompleteexplanation Satellite-based analyses provide compelling evi- dence for a top–down dynamical driver. Consistent with this view, Tselioudis et al [9] decomposed the global shortwave cloud radiative effect trend (+0.45 ± 0.09 W m−2 decade−1 over the period 2001–2024) and showed that the resulting con- traction of high-reflectivity cloud regimes accounts for ∼0.37 W m−2 decade−1, or roughly 80%, of the observed increase in absorbed solar radiation. Crucially, Tselioudiset al[9] identified this contrac- tion not as an isolated mechanism, but as the outcome of specific large-scale dynamical shifts: the narrowing of the intertropical convergence zone and the pole- ward shift of the mid-latitude storm tracks. Rather than challenging the well-established dis- tinction between high-cloud longwave warming and low-cloud shortwave cooling, this perspective high- lights how recent dynamical shifts have systematically reduced the prevalence of the most reflective low- cloud regimes, thereby enhancing shortwave absorp- tion under clearer-sky conditions. As Tselioudiset al [9]. conclude, ‘the storm cloud contraction is found to be the main contributor to the observed increase of the Earth’s solar absorption during the 21st cen- tury’ (p. 1). This contraction is most pronounced in the mid-latitude storm tracks of both hemispheres 2 Environ. Res. Lett.21(2026) 141002 G Bellocchiet al and the expanding subtropics, where the poleward migration of cloud systems exposes larger areas of the underlying ocean and land surfaces to incom- ing solar radiation. In doing so, cloud contraction does not replace surface controls but increases their radiative influence by reducing atmospheric masking and enhancing the climatic leverage of surface albedo [1, 6, 9]. 3.Surfacealbedo:theoverlookedyet strategicclimatelever If the atmosphere acts as the radiative ‘shutter’ of the Earth system, the surface functions as its pri- mary ‘absorber’ . Gottschalket al[10] show that land- use and management practices routinely alter sur- face albedo by 0.05–0.20, magnitudes capable of pro- ducing substantial regional radiative forcing. Surface reflectivity spans a diverse spectrum, from dark wet soils (∼0.05) and forests ( ∼0.05–0.15) to grass- lands (∼0.16–0.30), croplands ( ∼0.15–0.25), and dry sands (∼0.45). High-latitude and high-altitude regions are dominated for much of the year by fresh snow (∼0.90), while urban environments range from ∼0.10 to 0.28, with high-albedo cool-roof coat- ings reaching ∼0.5–0.6. In contrast to stochastic atmospheric circulation, surface reflectivity is directly manageable and, therefore, policy-relevant. Its cli- matic importance thus depends not only on its intrin- sic reflectivity but also on the degree of atmospheric masking by clouds. Practices such as cover cropping, residue reten- tion (the practice of leaving crop stalks and leaves on the field after harvest to act as a light-colored protec- tive layer), and the deployment of high-albedo urban materials can measurably increase surface reflectivity. However, these interventions are not unambiguously beneficial, and a central paradox remains as stated by Gottschalk [10]: ‘biogeophysical effects on climate associated with changes in surface albedo do not nec- essarily have the same sign, i.e. cooling or warming’ (p. 2). These effects often oppose one another. For example, afforestation may sequester carbon (a bio- geochemical cooling) while simultaneously darken- ing the surface and enhancing solar absorption (a biogeophysical warming). This tension highlights the need for a radiative-equivalent framework in which carbon management and albedo governance are eval- uated jointly rather than in isolation. 4.Echoesfromdeeptime:Mediterranean proxiesforclimatesensitivity Multi-centennial reconstructions of Mediterranean cloudiness provide essential contexts for interpreting recent trends. Diodatoet al[11] reconstructed west- ern Mediterranean cloudiness over the 969–2022 CE period, revealing a gradual increase in cloud cover throughout the early part of the second millennium. This trend culminated in a cloud cover peak around 1600 CE (coinciding with the coldest phase of the Little Ice Age), before entering a persistent long- term decline toward unprecedented modern minima. A complementary reconstruction (1500–2022 CE) across the broader Mediterranean [12] shows a pro- gressive decline beginning in the 17th century, fol- lowed by a post-early-19th century shift toward greater multidecadal variability and sustained lower levels. Notably, this transition began during the later phase of the Little Ice Age, indicating that the onset of cloud decline preceded the significant anthro- pogenic greenhouse forcing. This early onset likely reflects a synergistic unmasking of the land surface, driven by the combined effects of natural variabil- ity and historical land-use changes. Natural contribu- tions include post-volcanic recovery following major eruptions (e.g. Tambora in 1815), periods of elevated solar activity, and positive phases of the Atlantic mul- tidecadal oscillation (AMO). On the land-use side, peak European deforestation between approximately 1750 and 1850 reduced forest cover, which altered regional albedo, decreased evapotranspiration, and weakened moisture recycling. The resulting decline in convective cloud formation thinned the atmospheric cloud shield, exposing the surface to greater incom- ing solar radiation well before greenhouse-gas forcing intensified [13, 14]. Satellite-era observations (1982–2022) refine this historical picture. They reveal a marked decline in annual cloud cover alongside increasing tempera- tures, though with significant regional heterogeneity. Hotspots such as Sicily and northern Tunisia exhibit reductions approaching 10% per decade, coincident with a systemic shift around 2001. This period is characterized by altered spatial structures, variance distributions, and evolving connections to North Atlantic circulation anomalies (thermohaline pro- cesses). Conversely, previously reported heliospheric associations (e.g. links to galactic cosmic rays) have become heterogeneous [15]. These changes suggest a reorganization of coupled ocean–atmosphere vari- ability, potentially amplified by anthropogenic forc- ing but not solely driven by it. These long-term declines unfold against a back- drop of well-established natural oscillatory influ- ences. Solar variability and large-scale modes such as the AMO contribute to the ∼60 year multi- decadal signals evident in Mediterranean cloudiness. Planetary gravitational harmonics may also modu- late solar activity in ways that align with these quasi- periodic patterns, though the underlying mecha- nisms remain debated [16]. Y et it is against this oscil- latory background that a striking paradox emerges: the Mediterranean climate system is now behaving in ways inconsistent with historical controls. As Diodato et al [11] noted, ‘recent decades have witnessed an 3 Environ. Res. Lett.21(2026) 141002 G Bellocchiet al unprecedented coupling of intense solar activity, high temperatures, and the lowest cloud cover on record’ (p. 1). Since∼2001, cloud dynamics appear increas- ingly decoupled from historical oscillations and more strongly governed by large-scale circulation shifts [9, 15]. This points to a transition from quasi-periodic variability toward a sustained structural reduction in reflective cloud cover, with far-reaching implications for shortwave albedo feedbacks, regional climate sen- sitivity, and future warming trajectories. 5.Coupledcontrolsonradiation:from top–downtobottom-up The apparent separation between circulation-driven change and internal variability dissolves when the sys- tem is viewed as a set of coupled controls operat- ing across three interacting layers (figure2), where circulation-mediated cloud changes arise from inter- acting layers, radiative adjustments, and modes of variability [17]. First, large-scale atmospheric cir- culation (top–down) shapes the spatial extent, per- sistence, and regime transitions of cloud fields [9]. Second, aerosol-cloud microphysics (intermedi- ate) modulate cloud optical thickness and radiative efficiency [11]. Third, surface albedo (bottom-up) determines the fraction of incoming solar flux that is ultimately absorbed or reflected [10]. T ogether, these layers form a tightly coupled radiative system in which circulation sets the stage, microphysics tunes the optical properties, and sur- face reflectivity modulates the final partitioning of absorbed energy. This integrated perspective is essen- tial for diagnosing recent anomalies and anticipat- ing evolving climate sensitivity. In the intermedi- ate layer, and considering only cloudy-sky condi- tions, we distinguish two regimes. Optically thick clouds (typical of cloudy skies with high aerosol loading or dense cloud columns) enhance shortwave reflection and produce a net cooling effect. Optically thin clouds (occurring under reduced aerosol load- ing or in thinner cloud layers) permit greater trans- mission of solar radiation to the surface. Clear-sky conditions follow different physical principles: there, aerosol optical depth and molecular scattering gov- ern transmission independently of cloud processes [18], and are thus treated separately from the cloudy- sky optical thickness comparison above. For exam- ple, marine stratocumulus clouds exert a strong net cooling effect by efficiently reflecting incoming solar radiation, while deep convective clouds tend to pro- duce a net warming effect by trapping outgoing longwave radiation more effectively than they reflect shortwave radiation [17]. As cloud cover declines, a larger fraction of incoming solar radiation reaches the surface, increasing the radiative leverage of sur- face reflectivity. Mediterranean observations illus- trate how this shift amplifies nonlinear feedbacks: reduced cloudiness intensifies surface heating, alters evapotranspiration patterns, and reshapes boundary- layer dynamics. Specifically, this can trigger a pro- posed ‘humidity paradox’ , consistent with estab- lished land–atmosphere coupling mechanisms. In this regime, CO2-driven land-atmosphere interac- tions promote boundary-layer drying that inhibits cloud formation over land, reinforcing a positive feedback between reduced cloudiness, increased solar absorption, and perturbations to the surface water balance [ 19]. Although cloud attenuation varies across wavelengths, clearer-sky conditions generally increase the transmission of incoming shortwave radiation, enhancing surface absorption [20]. This asymmetry strengthens net surface energy gain without requiring strong spectral selectivity in cloud transmission. In this regime, wavelength- dependent surface emissivity may play a secondary but increasingly relevant role in regulating outgo- ing longwave radiation, particularly under dry and cloud-free conditions [21]. However, under increas- ingly clear-sky conditions, surface albedo becomes an increasingly influential control on the shortwave component of the planetary energy balance because diminishing cloud masking enhances the radiative sensitivity of the underlying surface. At the planetary scale, however, changes in cloud properties remain a major driver of TOA shortwave variability, particu- larly over oceans where surface albedo varies relatively little. Thus, cloud contraction and surface albedo should be viewed as complementary, dynamically coupled controls rather than competing explanations. As cloud cover declines, the climate system moves toward a state in which relatively small changes in sur- face albedo exert a disproportionately large influence on the surface energy balance. Order-of-magnitude estimates suggest that a 1% absolute increase in global planetary albedo could correspond to a cooling on the order of ∼1 ◦C [22], although the exact sensi- tivity depends on atmospheric state, cloud feedbacks, and spatial heterogeneity. Consistent with this frame- work, recent attribution studies further show that regional changes in cloud properties are often the primary driver of TOA shortwave albedo variability, while surface reflectivity modulates the realized radia- tive response under clearer-sky conditions [23]. 6.Synergies,trade-offs,andpolicy relevance Multiple land-atmosphere processes can combine additively, but their joint effects are rarely linear. Synergies are common: cover crops can simulta- neously enhance surface reflectivity and sequester carbon [24], and cool roofs mitigate both urban heat islands and building energy demand [25]. These inter- actions highlight the need of integrated, region- specific assessments that evaluate biogeophysical (albedo) and biogeochemical (carbon) effects as a sin- gle coupled system. Progress is constrained, however, by persistent uncertainties in how these processes unfold across space and time. The magnitude and durability of albedo changes vary widely across land-use types and management practices, while the sensitivity of low-cloud regimes to surface forcing depends on the prevailing circulation state. Earth system mod- els still tend to represent cloud-surface coupling in a fragmented manner, and long-term, high-resolution albedo records remain insufficient to resolve low- frequency variability or emerging regional trends. These limitations complicate efforts to quantify the climatic consequences of land-surface interventions and to anticipate their interactions with atmospheric reorganization. Translating this understanding into practice requires three mutually reinforcing operational pillars: monitoring, constraint, and optimization. Monitoring depends on operational satellite products (e.g. MODIS, CERES, VIIRS successors) capable of continuously tracking cloud fields and surface reflectivity at policy-relevant scales [3], providing the radiative context needed to detect emerging anomalies. Constraint relies on proxy-based cli- mate reconstructions that preserve low-frequency natural variability [26], anchoring modern observa- tions within a multi-centennial baseline to distin- guish structural shifts from oscillatory background behavior. Optimization involves decision-support tools that jointly evaluate carbon sequestration and surface-reflectivity outcomes [ 27], enabling prac- titioners to maximize climate benefits while mini- mizing unintended trade-offs across heterogeneous landscapes. T ogether, these elements define a research agenda that improves atmospheric representation while actively managing surface properties, a dual strategy that offers a pragmatic pathway to slow the accumulation of radiative energy. 7.Conclusionandoutlook The contraction of cloud regimes, the long-term decline of Mediterranean cloudiness, and widespread shifts in surface albedo are not isolated phenomena. 5 Environ. Res. Lett.21(2026) 141002 G Bellocchiet al Rather, they are interconnected components of a broader transition in the Earth’s energy balance: a rapidly dimming planetary mirror. As skies clear, a larger fraction of incoming shortwave radiation reaches the surface, increasing the climatic influence of land and ocean reflectivity. Rather than repre- senting competing explanations, cloud contraction and surface albedo act as dynamically coupled con- trols, with changes in cloud cover determining the extent to which surface reflectivity influences the planetary radiation budget. This systems perspective is further reinforced by recent satellite observations showing that Earth’s planetary albedo is maintained through compensating interactions among atmo- spheric circulation, cloud radiative effects, and clear- sky surface reflectivity, highlighting the inherently coupled nature of the shortwave radiation budget across hemispheric scales [28]. Under these clearer- sky conditions, surface properties exert growing lever- age on regional and potentially global energy bud- gets. Integrating this coupling into climate science and policy offers three distinct advantages: it sharpens attribution by forcing joint consideration of circu- lation changes, internal variability, and surface pro- cesses; it exposes critical gaps in Earth system mod- els, demanding better representation of both cloud dynamics and low-frequency regional shifts; and it elevates surface-albedo management from a niche concept to a vital, complementary mitigation lever alongside carbon reduction. These insights demand a comprehensive approach to climate governance that actively manages both atmospheric composition and surface reflectivity. By refining our understanding of atmospheric processes while strategically optimiz- ing land-surface properties, society can develop more robust pathways to slow the accumulation of radia- tive energy. Navigating this emerging era of clear-sky amplification requires us to treat the Earth’s surface not merely as a recipient of climate change, but as an active participant in its stabilization.
