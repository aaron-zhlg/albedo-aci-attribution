Wall6,7, Paulo Ceppi 8 & Andrew Gettelman 4 Earth’s energy imbalance has increased markedly over the past two decades, reaching a record in 2023. Both the long-term trend and year-to-year variations are linked to reduced reﬂection of sunlight by low-level clouds, which is pronounced over Northern Hemisphere oceans. However, the causes of these cloud changes and their implications for future Earth system evolution are unknown, raising concerns about a stronger-than-expected cloud feedback. Here we quantify the meteorological factors behind interannual cloud-radiative anomalies, several of which aligned to produce the extreme 2023 value. These meteorological variations are superposed on a background of declining sulfate aerosol concentrations, contributing to a sustained decrease in cloud reﬂection over the past 22 years. The resulting constraints on cloud feedback and aerosol forcing are consistent with previous studies, supporting an equilibrium climate sensitivity near 3∘C (likely range 2.7–4.1∘C). Thus, recent observations do not indicate an emerging stronger cloud feedback or underestimated future warming. Recent studies reveal a marked increase in Earth’s energy imbalance (EEI) over the past 25 years, driven by the steady decline in planetary albedo1,2. Notably, Earth’s albedo reached a record minimum in 20233, coinciding with a large spike in global mean surface temperature4. Global climate models generally struggle to simulate EEI trends as large as observed, par- ticularly in the shortwave5–7. This may implicate biases in model repre- sentation of key physics governing cloud feedback or aerosol-cloud interactions, forcing errors, or some combination of both. A substantial portion of both the trend and the extreme albedo anomaly in 2023 stems from decreases in low-level cloud amount or reﬂectivity1,8, but the exact reasons for these cloud changes remain unknown3. Decadal cloud trends and recent extremes result from internal climate variability and forced responses involving diverse drivers. Secular increases in greenhouse gases drive warming that may reduce cloud reﬂection through positive cloud feedbacks9,10, while simultaneous decreases in aerosol concentrations11–14 may reduce cloud reﬂection through aerosol- cloud interactions. Recent work has highlighted the potential role of new shipping fuel regulations in reducing sulfate aerosol loading15–17,b u tt h e ensuing cloud-radiative impacts remain uncertain. Recent trends and extremes in EEI, planetary albedo, and surface temperature, along with models’apparent inability to capture them, have prompted speculation that cloud feedbacks, aerosol-cloud interactions, or climate sensitivity are currently underestimated3,18.H e r ew es h o wt h a t observed trends and extreme cloud-induced radiation anomalies can be reproduced via“cloud-controlling factor” (CCF) analysis using observed sensitivities of cloud-radiative effects to meteorology and aerosols. Its suc- cess strengthens conﬁdence in the CCF framework, while enabling attri- bution of cloud responses to speciﬁc controlling factors. We then show that observational constraints on cloud feedback and the effective radiative forcing from aerosol-cloud interactions (ERFaci) derived using this updated uniﬁed set of CCF coefﬁcients are consistent with previous studies and with a moderate equilibrium climate sensitivity (ECS), in agreement with existing consensus. Hence, we conclude that recent observations do not imply that larger-than-expected cloud feedback or ERFaci are emerging in nature or that future warming has been underestimated. Conclusions Observationally-derived coefﬁcients quantifying low-cloud sensitivity to meteorology and aerosols, multiplied by observed changes in these factors, successfully captures observed trends inlow-cloud radiative effects over the past 22 years and recent record anomalies. These coefﬁcients, when com- bined with model-predicted changes in meteorology and aerosols, yield observationally-constrained estimates of indirect aerosol forcing and low- cloud feedback within the range of previous studies, implying climate sen- sitivity near 3∘C, consistent with community assessments. Contrary to the speculation put forward in several prominent studies, recent trends and extremes do not indicate stronger-than-expected aerosol forcing, cloud feedbacks, or climate sensitivity; rather they are consistent with known sensitivities of clouds to their environment. While cloud-radiative anoma- lies and trends over the past 22 years are within expectations, continued global monitoring of clouds and radiation remains crucial to assess how Earth’s energy budget is impacted by the likely larger warming, continued aerosol reductions, and evolving surface temperature patterns of future years. We perform our regression analysis iteratively over each year in the record as follows: Both CCFi and Rcld anomalies areﬁrst detrended. For a given target year, we remove that year and 2 years on either side of it from the dataset (a total of 5 years surrounding the target year). For years near the end of the record, we remove the available 5 adjacent years (e.g., for 2023, we remove 2020–2024). Using this subset of CCFi and Rcld data with a 5-year period withheld, we perform ordinary least-squares multilinear regression to derive∂Rcld/∂CCFi,w h i c hq u a n t iﬁes the sensitivities ofRcld to each CCFi holding the other CCFs constant. This process is done iteratively over each complete year in the data record (2003–2024), such that coefﬁcients that will be used in the prediction of a given year are derived without “knowledge” of a 5-year period encompassing the target year or of theunderlying trend in the data. Coef- ﬁcient maps are qualitatively similar across target years (in each case withholding different 5-year windows), indicating that the sensitivities are largely invariant with time, an important prerequisite for CCF analysis. Results are also largely insensitive to the withholding window length, but coefﬁcient uncertainties increase as more years are withheld. Note that the raw anomalies including the trend are used when makingΔRcld predictions with these coefﬁcients; only the dataset used toﬁt the multilinear regression model is detrended.
